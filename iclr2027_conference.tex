\begin{abstract}
Recent advancements in Reinforcement Learning (RL) have heavily adopted World Models and Diffusion-based techniques; however, they often suffer from prohibitive computational costs. In this paper, we first investigate the common assumption that the performance bottleneck of existing models lies primarily in their representation capacity (e.g., poor image reconstruction). Through rigorous probing analysis, we provide evidence suggesting that existing lightweight encoders can already capture robust object-level information. This implies that representation capacity may not be the sole limitation, prompting us to investigate other structural candidates. Among these, we identify the optimization process of the Actor-Critic architecture as a major, independent bottleneck, characterized by extreme performance variance across different random seeds. To mitigate this specific issue, we propose FLASH (Fast Latent Anchor Search with Hashing), a lightweight and efficient plug-and-play module that naturally aids Actor-Critic optimization. By seamlessly integrating into various RL baselines—ranging from lightweight encoder-based models to heavy diffusion-based models—FLASH elevates their performance significantly while preserving their original frames per second (FPS). Crucially, our approach acts as a powerful "Exploitation Amplifier," maximizing the evaluation scores of seeds that successfully explore, all with negligible computational overhead.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Recently, the integration of Diffusion models into Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become increasingly popular, yielding strong performance in various benchmarks \todo{DIAMOND 등 Diffusion 기반 RL 최신 논문 인용}. However, this trend has brought about a significant drawback: massive computational costs and high latency during both training and inference. This raises a fundamental question: \textit{Are these heavy computational overheads truly necessary, or are we addressing the wrong bottleneck?}

In this work, we suggest that the current singular focus on enhancing representation quality through heavy generative models may overlook other critical components of the RL pipeline. By observing extreme performance variance across different random seeds in standard setups, we hypothesize that the inherent instability in the Actor-Critic optimization process itself constitutes a major, independent bottleneck. 

Our contributions are threefold:
First, we provide analytical evidence suggesting the sufficiency of existing lightweight representations in certain environments, demonstrating that poor visual reconstruction does not necessarily equate to a loss of underlying semantic information.
Second, having established that representations are not the sole limitation, we identify the Actor-Critic optimization process as a distinct and major bottleneck hindering RL performance, evidenced by extreme seed-to-seed variance.
Finally, to mitigate this specific optimization bottleneck, we propose FLASH, an efficient retrieval mechanism that augments the Actor-Critic architecture. By effectively identifying and retrieving pivotal past experiences, our method stabilizes learning and acts as a powerful exploitation amplifier, achieving state-of-the-art results with minimal computational overhead.

\section{Are Heavy Representations Necessary?: Decoding the Sufficiency of Encoders}
\label{sec:representation}

\subsection{The Myth of Reconstruction Quality}
A common mistake in recent model-based RL research is the assumption that blurry decoded images imply a lack of information in the latent space. We argue that this is merely a rendering failure of the decoder.

As qualitative evidence, we observe the \textit{temporal persistence of objects} during the \textbf{open-loop imagination phase} in models like DreamerV3 and Drama \todo{해당 논문 인용}. During this phase, the model predicts future states solely based on its internal transition dynamics, without access to real observations. For instance, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:temporal_persistence}, during an imagined trajectory, a clearly rendered object (e.g., a fish) may blur or disappear from the decoded frame for a timestep (e.g., $t+1$), only to reappear perfectly in its expected trajectory subsequently (e.g., $t+2$). Since the model has no external input to "rediscover" the fish at $t+2$, this phenomenon definitively proves that the ontological information of the object was never lost in the latent states; the transition model successfully propagated it through the sequence, and it was simply a rendering failure of the decoder. Therefore, relying on reconstruction quality is a deceptive metric for evaluating the true capacity of an encoder.

\subsection{Probing the Latent Space}
To verify the latent state $z$ contains important yet small particles like diver, we conducted a probing experiment on the Seaquest environment, known for its extreme sparse rewards and tiny objects. We detached the pre-trained encoder (stopping gradient flow) and trained a lightweight MLP to predict ground-truth RAM states directly from $z$. Crucially, we evaluated a suboptimal seed—one that merely hunts fish and never learns the complex behavior of collecting 6 divers and surfacing.

Our results reveal that the MLP can predict the underlying game state with astonishing accuracy, even from this "failed" agent. Specifically, it achieved \todo{화면 하단 잠수부 개수 분류 정확도 수치} accuracy for counting the number of divers at the bottom, \todo{화면 상 잠수부 존재 여부 분류 정확도 수치} accuracy for detecting the presence of a diver on screen, and a mean squared error of \todo{x,y 좌표 예측 오차 수치} for predicting the exact $(x, y)$ coordinates of the diver. This confirms that the latent space already encapsulates perfect semantic information, irrespective of the decoder's visual output or the actor's behavioral performance.

\subsection{Robustness to Spatial Noise: Refuting the Blurring Hypothesis}
Recent works advocating for Diffusion models \todo{DIAMOND 논문 인용} hypothesize that standard encoders fail because they blur or "entangle" spatial information. To refute this, we injected heavy random noise into the background spaces of the original images, leaving only the diver's position intact.

If the entanglement hypothesis were true, the extreme noise in other spatial locations would entangle with the diver's features, degrading the probing accuracy. However, our probing MLP, taking the noise-injected $z$ as input, still maintained \todo{노이즈 주입 후 유지된 잠수부 위치/개수 정확도 수치} accuracy. This demonstrates that the encoder possesses robust disentanglement capabilities, completely debunking the claim that spatial entanglement is the root cause of RL failures.

\section{Beyond Representations: The Actor-Critic Optimization Bottleneck}
\label{sec:bottleneck}

\subsection{Insufficient Exploitation of Successful Trajectories}

We observe that even when some seeds discover high-reward trajectories during training, the Actor-Critic often fails to fully capitalize on them, resulting in evaluation scores far below the potential suggested by its best episodes.

\subsection{The Challenge of Delayed Rewards and Value Spikes}
Environments like Seaquest or Frostbite often require the agent to complete complex sub-goals (e.g., collecting multiple divers or building an igloo) before receiving a massive reward. In such scenarios, the agent may occasionally stumble upon a breakthrough during random exploration, causing a sudden, massive spike in the true state value. Traditional Actor-Critic architectures struggle to efficiently stabilize and learn from these rare, high-variance value spikes, often losing the critical exploratory signal in the noise of standard transitions.

\section{Proposed Method: FLASH (Fast Latent Anchor Search with Hashing)}
\label{sec:method}

To directly address the fundamental bottleneck in Actor-Critic optimization, we introduce FLASH.

\subsection{Episodic Memory and Retrieval with FLASH}
\label{subsec:flash_method}

Instead of relying solely on parametric gradient updates, FLASH maintains a lightweight episodic memory indexed by Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH) and retrieves structurally similar past contexts to directly augment the Actor-Critic training batch. The pipeline consists of four stages: \textbf{(1) Hashing \& Indexing}, \textbf{(2) Surprise-based Anchor Triggering}, \textbf{(3) Lazy Retrieval with Re-verification}, and \textbf{(4) In-batch Injection with Inverse-Group Weighting}. We describe each stage below and summarize the complete procedure in Algorithm~\ref{alg:flash}. We additionally illustrate the overall architecture in Figure~\ref{fig:flash_pipeline}.

\paragraph{Stage 1: Hashing \& Indexing.}
At every environment step, the world model encoder $f_\theta$ maps the current observation $o_t$ to a categorical latent representation $z_t = f_\theta(o_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, where $d = K \times C$ with $K$ categorical variables each of $C$ classes (in practice $K{=}32, C{=}32$, so $d{=}1024$). We apply an LSH function $h: \mathbb{R}^d \to \{0,1,\ldots,2^B{-}1\}$ to produce a discrete hash key:
\begin{equation}
h(z_t) = \sum_{i=1}^{B} \mathbf{1}\!\left[\left(W_{\text{proj}}^\top (z_t - \mu)\right)_i > 0\right] \cdot 2^{i-1},
\label{eq:lsh}
\end{equation}
where $B$ is the number of hash bits, $W_{\text{proj}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times B}$ is the projection matrix, and $\mu$ is the centering vector. Initially, $W_{\text{proj}}$ is a random Gaussian projection and $\mu = \mathbf{0}$; during \textit{Global Rebuild} (described below), both are updated via PCA on the current buffer's latent distribution to align the hash hyperplanes with the principal axes of variation.

Each transition pointer $(p_t, e)$---where $p_t$ is the circular buffer index and $e$ is the environment index---is inserted into a hash bucket $\mathcal{B}_{h(z_t)}$. If a pointer's hash key changes (due to Global Rebuild), it is transparently migrated. All bucket operations (insert, delete, lookup) run in amortized $\mathcal{O}(1)$.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Surprise-based Anchor Triggering.}
During each world model training step, FLASH monitors the temporal-difference (TD) error and value difference to detect ``surprise'' events---moments where the agent's value estimate changes abruptly, indicating a potentially pivotal experience. Given a batch of sampled trajectories with value estimates $V(s_t)$ from the critic, we compute:
\begin{align}
\delta_t^{\text{TD}} &= \left| r_t + \gamma \, V(s_{t+1})(1 - d_t) - V(s_t) \right|, \label{eq:td_error} \\
\Delta V_t &= V(s_t) - V(s_{t-1}), \label{eq:value_diff}
\end{align}
where $r_t$ is the reward, $\gamma$ is the discount factor, and $d_t$ is the termination flag. To normalize across environments with varying reward scales, we maintain per-environment exponential moving averages (EMA) of the mean and variance:
\begin{align}
\bar{\mu}_e &\leftarrow (1-\alpha)\bar{\mu}_e + \alpha \cdot \mathbb{E}[\delta^{\text{TD}}_{e}], \quad
\bar{\sigma}^2_e \leftarrow (1-\alpha)(\bar{\sigma}^2_e + \alpha \cdot \text{Var}[\delta^{\text{TD}}_{e}]), \label{eq:ema_update}
\end{align}
and analogously for $\Delta V$. The combined surprise metric is:
\begin{equation}
S_t = \text{ReLU}\!\left(\frac{\delta^{\text{TD}}_t - \bar{\mu}_e}{\sqrt{\bar{\sigma}^2_e} + \epsilon}\right) \cdot \text{softplus}\!\left(\frac{\Delta V_t - \bar{\mu}^{V}_e}{\sqrt{\bar{\sigma}^{2,V}_e} + \epsilon}\right).
\label{eq:surprise}
\end{equation}
This formulation fires only when \textit{both} the TD-error is abnormally large \textit{and} the value is rising, capturing precisely the moments where a high-reward sub-trajectory is first discovered. For each trajectory sequence in the batch, when the maximum surprise score across its time steps exceeds the threshold ($\max_{t \in \text{seq}} S_t > \tau_z$), the corresponding buffer pointer (offset by $\Delta_{\text{anchor}}$ steps to align with the causal context window) is registered as an \textit{active anchor} $a = (p_{t^*+\Delta_\text{anchor}},\, e)$ where $t^* = \text{argmax}_{t \in \text{seq}} S_t$, in a FIFO queue $\mathcal{A}$.

\paragraph{Stage 3: Lazy Retrieval with Re-verification.}
At each imagination step (where the agent generates imagined trajectories for policy training), FLASH pops up to $M$ anchors from $\mathcal{A}$. For each anchor $a$ with stored hash key $k_a$, we sample up to $m \times (n{-}1)$ candidate pointers from bucket $\mathcal{B}_{k_a}$ (excluding the anchor itself). To combat \textit{representation drift}---the gradual shift in hash keys as $f_\theta$ evolves---we perform \textbf{lazy re-verification}: each candidate's single-frame observation is re-encoded through the \textit{current} world model, and only candidates whose re-computed hash key matches $k_a$ are retained. This yields a set of verified neighbors $\mathcal{N}_a$ with $|\mathcal{N}_a| \leq n$, including the anchor itself.

If the average hit rate (fraction of candidates passing re-verification) drops below a threshold $\eta$, a \textbf{Global Rebuild} is triggered: all hash buckets are cleared, the PCA projection $W_{\text{proj}}$ and mean $\mu$ are recomputed from a fresh encoding of the entire replay buffer, and all transitions are re-hashed. This ensures the hash structure stays aligned with the evolving representation, subject to a cooldown period to amortize cost.

\paragraph{Stage 4: In-batch Injection with Inverse-Group Weighting.}
For each retrieved group $\mathcal{N}_a$, we extract the preceding $L$-step context window of observations and actions from the replay buffer (validating that no episode termination occurs within the window). Let $N_{\text{ret}} = \sum_{a} |\mathcal{N}_a|$ denote the total number of retrieved contexts (including both anchors and their matched neighbors). These are concatenated with randomly-sampled contexts to form the imagination batch:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_{\text{imagine}} = \underbrace{\{(o_{t-L:t},\, a_{t-L:t})\}_{i=1}^{N_\text{rand}}}_{\text{random samples}} \;\cup\; \underbrace{\bigcup_{a \in \text{popped}} \{(o^{(j)}_{t-L:t},\, a^{(j)}_{t-L:t})\}_{j \in \mathcal{N}_a}}_{\text{retrieved contexts (anchors + neighbors)}},
\label{eq:batch_compose}
\end{equation}
where $N_{\text{rand}} = \max\big(0,\, N_{\text{total}} - M_{\text{valid}}\big)$, and $M_{\text{valid}} \leq M$ is the number of anchor groups that yield at least one valid context. Because we substitute $M_{\text{valid}}$ random samples with $N_{\text{ret}}$ retrieved contexts (where $N_{\text{ret}} \geq M_{\text{valid}}$), the physical batch size actually expands to $N_{\text{total}} - M_{\text{valid}} + N_{\text{ret}}$.

To prevent any single anchor group from dominating the gradient, each member within group $\mathcal{N}_a$ receives an \textbf{inverse-group weight} $w_j = 1/|\mathcal{N}_a|$, while every random sample receives $w=1$. This weighting scheme serves two critical purposes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preventing Gradient Domination:} Since members of a retrieved group are sampled from the same hash bucket and could be structurally similar, assigning a full unit weight to each would cause redundantly similar situations to dominate the gradient update. Inverse-group weighting acts as a normalizer to prevent this over-representation.
    \item \textbf{Maintaining Learning Speed Ratio:} It ensures each group's total weight contribution remains exactly $\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_a} w_j = 1$, equivalent to a single random sample. Consequently, the total weight sum across the entire augmented batch is strictly conserved:
\end{itemize}
\begin{equation}
W_{\text{sum}} = N_{\text{rand}} + M_{\text{valid}} = N_{\text{total}}.
\label{eq:weight_sum}
\end{equation}
All Actor-Critic losses---policy, value, and entropy---are normalized by this constant sum:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AC}} = \frac{1}{W_{\text{sum}}} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{imagine}}} w_i \left[\mathcal{L}_{\text{actor}}(s_i, a_i) + \mathcal{L}_{\text{critic}}(s_i) - \beta \, \mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot | s_i))\right].
\label{eq:weighted_loss}
\end{equation}
By keeping $W_{\text{sum}}$ strictly equal to the original $N_{\text{total}}$, we ensure that the learning speed ratio between the world model and the actor-critic remains perfectly balanced, even as the physical batch size expands to absorb the retrieved pivotal experiences.

\paragraph{Warmup Curriculum.}
During an initial warmup phase (the first $T_{\text{warm}}$ environment steps), the EMA statistics accumulate but no anchors are triggered and no retrieval occurs. This prevents noisy early-stage value estimates from corrupting the anchor selection process. Retrieval is activated only after the world model and value function have stabilized sufficiently.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{FLASH: Fast Latent Anchor Search with Hashing}
\label{alg:flash}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Replay buffer $\mathcal{R}$, world model encoder $f_\theta$, critic $V_\psi$, hash function $h(\cdot)$
\REQUIRE Hyperparameters: hash bits $B$, z-score threshold $\tau_z$, warmup steps $T_{\text{warm}}$, context length $L$, max anchors $M$, multiplier $m$, target $n$, rebuild threshold $\eta$, cooldown $C$
\STATE Initialize hash buckets $\{\mathcal{B}_k\}$, anchor queue $\mathcal{A} \leftarrow \emptyset$, EMA statistics
\FOR{each environment step $t$}
    \STATE Encode $z_t \leftarrow f_\theta(o_t)$, compute $k_t \leftarrow h(z_t)$
    \STATE Insert pointer $(p_t, e)$ into $\mathcal{B}_{k_t}$
\ENDFOR
\FOR{each world model training step (after warmup)}
    \STATE Compute batch TD-errors $\delta^{\text{TD}}_t$ and value-differences $\Delta V_t$
    \STATE Update EMA statistics via Eq.~\eqref{eq:ema_update}
    \STATE Compute surprise $S_t$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:surprise}
    \IF{$\max_t S_t > \tau_z$}
        \STATE Push anchor $(p_{t^*+\Delta_\text{anchor}}, e)$ into $\mathcal{A}$
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\FOR{each imagination step (policy training)}
    \STATE Pop up to $M$ anchors from $\mathcal{A}$
    \FOR{each anchor $a$ with key $k_a$}
        \STATE Sample candidates from $\mathcal{B}_{k_a}$, re-encode, keep matches $\to \mathcal{N}_a$
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Compose augmented batch $\mathcal{D}_{\text{imagine}}$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:batch_compose}
    \STATE Train Actor-Critic with weighted loss (Eq.~\eqref{eq:weighted_loss})
    \IF{avg hit rate $< \eta$ and cooldown elapsed}
        \STATE \textbf{Global Rebuild}: PCA $\to$ update $(W_{\text{proj}}, \mu)$, re-hash all $\mathcal{R}$
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Theoretical Intuition: Gradient Acceleration via In-Batch Retrieval}
A core strength of FLASH is how it naturally accelerates optimization without introducing artificial contrastive biases. By simply retrieving historical states and injecting them into the current training batch alongside the current state, we construct a highly informative local gradient step. 

Intuitively, this in-batch proximity creates a bifurcated gradient effect:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Constructive Alignment:} If the current state and the retrieved state are genuinely similar, processing them simultaneously allows the network to smoothly align their value estimates, reinforcing the correct learning signal.
    \item \textbf{Destructive Separation (Anti-Aliasing):} If the states merely appear similar but represent different underlying game dynamics (perceptual aliasing), placing them in the exact same batch forces the network to differentiate them immediately. If such aliased states were processed in separate batches across different steps, their conflicting gradients would oscillate and cancel each other out, severely delaying convergence. By processing them simultaneously in the same forward-backward pass, the gradients actively push the network to separate their representations and value predictions in a single step.
\end{itemize}
Thus, simply altering the batch composition via retrieval naturally acts as a self-correcting regularizer that drastically speeds up optimization.

\label{fig:exploitation_amplifier}
\end{figure}

A key property of FLASH is its ability to act as an "Exploitation Amplifier." As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:exploitation_amplifier}, if a seed manages to achieve a high episode score (e.g., $>800$) even once during early exploration, our method aggressively exploits this trajectory, pulling the overall evaluation score up to converge near the maximum achieved episode score. Conversely, for seeds that completely fail to explore (e.g., $<300$ points), there are no high-quality past experiences to retrieve, resulting in no artificial performance gain. This demonstrates that FLASH does not magically solve hardcore exploration, but ensures that rare, successful exploratory signals are never lost and are optimally absorbed by the policy.

\subsection{Computational Efficiency: Negligible Overhead}
Because FLASH focuses solely on optimizing the Actor-Critic training rather than modifying the heavy representation models, it adds practically zero computational burden to the base model. 

The algorithmic time complexity is as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Insertion per frame: $\mathcal{O}(d \cdot B) \approx \mathcal{O}(1)$. The representation encoding cost $\mathcal{O}(|f_\theta|)$ is completely absorbed since the world model inherently computes the latent state during environment interaction. Because the latent dimension $d$ and hash bits $B$ are small fixed parameters, this operates in effectively constant time.
    \item Global Rebuild: $\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{buf}} d^2 + d^3 + N_{\text{buf}} d B) \approx \mathcal{O}(N_{\text{buf}})$. Even though PCA is computed over the entire replay buffer, the latent dimensions $d$ and hash bits $B$ are fixed, so the overall operation scales linearly only with the buffer size $N_{\text{buf}}$. Furthermore, Global Rebuild is not executed every step; it is only triggered occasionally when the lazy rebuild hit-rate drops, making its amortized cost negligibly small.
    \item Retrieval step: $\mathcal{O}\big(M m n (|f_\theta| + d \cdot B) + M n L\big) \approx \mathcal{O}(1)$. The maximum number of anchors $M$, candidates $m \times n$, and context length $L$ are strictly bounded hyperparameters. Thus, the verification and extraction process runs in constant $\mathcal{O}(1)$ time.
    \item Anchor Triggering: $\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{batch}} \cdot L_{\text{seq}}) \approx \mathcal{O}(1)$. Performing lightweight TD-error and EMA-based Z-score tracking over the imagined sequences requires no additional neural network forward passes. Since the batch size and sequence length are fixed, the overhead is constant per step.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
In this paper, we critique the recent trend of obsessing over visual representation quality in RL. We demonstrate that existing lightweight encoders already possess sufficient capacity, implying that representation learning is no longer the primary bottleneck. Recognizing that the Actor-Critic optimization itself acts as a crucial, independent bottleneck, we proposed FLASH, an efficient and low-overhead retrieval method. Our plug-and-play approach successfully amplifies rare exploratory successes and universally elevates the performance of existing RL baselines—including both lightweight and heavy diffusion-based models—while maintaining extreme computational efficiency.
